# IPA Writing Guidelines for Evidence Summaries

This file contains the official IPA writing guidelines that will be applied when
generating evidence summaries. The LLM will follow these guidelines to ensure
consistent, high-quality output that matches IPA's communication standards.
Based on: IPA Communications Team Writing Guidelines (February 2023).

## General Writing Principles

### Keep it short and simple

Attention spans are getting smaller and smaller. Short sentences are useful to
keep the reader's attention.

- Avoid one-paragraph sentences
- Each sentence should convey a single idea
- Do not ramble; focus on the main message
- Avoid fancy adjectives and adverbs when simpler alternatives are available
- **Use active voice** whenever possible (e.g., "Researchers conducted a study"
  not "A study was conducted")
- **Keep sentences clear and concise**: Aim for an average of 15-20 words per
  sentence

**Example:**

- ❌ **Needs improvement**: To support national-level policy development and
  program design in the places where we work, IPA leverages our evidence
  expertise to provide actionable lessons from relevant research by synthesizing
  evidence in clear terms and bringing key insights to inform decision-making.
- ✅ **Better**: IPA synthesizes evidence to provide key insights and actionable
  lessons for policy and program development.

### Write clearly and avoid jargon

The best way to be clear is to keep your audience in mind.

- **Write for a general audience**: Assume readers are educated but not
  specialists in the field
- Write so that your audience can understand your message without additional
  background
- **Avoid jargon and technical terms** unless necessary; explain them clearly on
  first use
- Use jargon carefully and only when writing for specialized audiences
- **Use concrete, specific language** rather than vague or abstract terms

**Example:**

- ❌ **Needs improvement**: We estimate the intent to treat effect at plot level
  to better understand adoption heterogeneity, particularly by gender of the
  plot manager.
- ✅ **Better**: The researchers analyzed how adoption rates differ at the plot
  level depending on the gender of the plot manager.

### Avoid unknown acronyms and abbreviations

- Spell out acronyms on first use: "Innovations for Poverty Action (IPA)"
- After first mention, use the acronym: "IPA"
- Use acronyms and abbreviations only if they are well known to your audience
- Avoid abbreviations that might not be universally understood
- For longer documents that use many acronyms, a complete list should be
  provided at the front
- Avoid the use of internal acronyms (such as PIs, SMT, P&R) in external
  communications

**Example:**

- ❌ **Needs improvement**: IPA's PIs will circle back to ADB re: the RCTs
  scenario.
- ✅ **Better**: IPA-affiliated researchers will contact the Asia Development
  Bank (ADB) again regarding the potential use of randomized controlled trials
  (RCTs).

### Use active voice instead of passive voice

Active voice uses fewer words, making the writing more understandable and often
more engaging.

- When writing in active voice, use: subject, then verb or verbal phrase, then
  object
- **Action-oriented**: Focus on what was done and what was found

**Example:**

- ❌ **Passive Voice**: Information was collected on migrants' demographic
  background.
- ✅ **Active Voice Preferred**: Surveyors collected information on the
  migrants' demographic background.
- ❌ **Passive Voice**: A randomized evaluation was conducted to assess the
  effectiveness of the program.
- ✅ **Active Voice Preferred**: Researchers conducted a randomized evaluation
  to assess the program's effectiveness.

### Avoid normative words and statements

IPA's research allows us to make statements based on evidence.

- **Evidence-based and objective**: Present findings neutrally without advocacy
- Avoid any unsupported claims
- Be careful with normative words that suggest we have an emotional attachment
  or preference (such as surprisingly, startlingly, disappointingly,
  encouragingly)

**Example:**

- ❌ **Needs improvement**: Encouragingly, cash transfer programs have been the
  single most effective mechanism to alleviate the devastating effects of
  extreme poverty.
- ✅ **Better**: Research suggests that cash transfer programs can be an
  effective mechanism to reduce the negative impacts of extreme poverty.

### Show, don't tell

IPA is all about evidence. Use it in your writing too.

- Rather than stating something for the reader to accept, show the evidence
- Use description and immersive details to write an engaging text
- Be careful: the line between storytelling and rambling is thin

**Examples:**

- ❌ **Tell**: The program had positive effects.

- ✅ **Show**: The program reduced child malnutrition rates by five percent.

- ❌ **Tell**: Data collection was challenging given the village conditions.

- ✅ **Show**: The village was located in a remote area. To collect data,
  surveyors had to take a road whose poor conditions turned a thirty-minute trip
  into a four-hour trip.

### Be consistent with your writing

Adopt consistency throughout:

- One tone throughout the text
- Consistent pronouns and verb tense
- Standard terminology
- Uniform spelling style (American English)
- Consistent punctuation
- One style for footnotes, citations, and references
- Consistent format (fonts, sizes, spacing, margins)

**Example:**

- ❌ **Needs improvement**: High value cash transfers - above 30 percent of mean
  annual income per capita, have been found to demonstrate differential impacts
  on households compared with small cash transfers.
- ✅ **Better**: High-value cash transfers, which account for more than 30
  percent of average annual income per capita, have a different impact on
  households than low-value cash transfers.

### Maintain a professional tone

- **Professional but accessible**: Maintain academic rigor while being readable
- IPA's tone is formal
- Stay away from slang, figures of speech, contractions, and abbreviated words
- Use "technology" instead of "tech"
- Avoid the use of first person (I, we, us) and second person (you)
- Use third person (IPA) instead
- **Respectful**: Use inclusive, non-discriminatory language

**Example:**

- ❌ **Needs improvement**: It's key for us to get decent stats.
- ✅ **Better**: It is important for IPA to develop rigorous evidence.

## Tone and Style

Following the *Associated Press Stylebook* (56th edition):

- Focus on consistency, clarity, accuracy, and brevity
- Use American spelling and punctuation, not British
- "labor" not "labour"
- Periods and commas go inside quotation marks
- Use *Merriam-Webster's Collegiate Dictionary* (11th edition) for spelling
- Use *Chicago Manual of Style* (17th Edition) for citations

## Technical Terms and Research Terminology

### Research Terminology

- **Use "evaluation"** instead of "experiment"
- **Use "participants" or "respondents"** rather than "subjects"
- **Use "randomized evaluation"** when addressing general audiences rather than
  "RCT"
- **Describe methodology** in accessible terms without sacrificing accuracy
- **Translate statistical significance**: Explain what "statistically
  significant" means in context

### Avoid "Treatment" and "Control" Language

- **Avoid**: "treatment" and "control" - use descriptive alternatives
- **Prefer**: "enrolled in the program" vs. "in the treatment group"
- **Prefer**: "did not receive the program" vs. "in the control group"
- **Prefer**: "students enrolled in the program" vs. "treatment students"

**Examples:**

- ❌ **Avoid**: Treatment group students outperformed control group students by
  20%.
- ✅ **Prefer**: Students enrolled in the program scored 20% higher than similar
  students who did not participate.

### Defining Technical Terms

- **Define on first use**: "randomized controlled trial (RCT)"
- Explain what methodology means in accessible language

**Examples:**

- ❌ **Avoid**: Using a DID approach with PSM, researchers found significant
  ATEs.

- ✅ **Prefer**: Researchers used statistical methods to compare changes over
  time between program participants and similar non-participants. They found
  that the program had significant positive effects.

- ❌ **Avoid**: The DID estimator showed...

- ✅ **Prefer**: Using difference-in-differences analysis (comparing changes
  over time)...

### Translating Statistics

- **Provide context for statistics**: Always explain what numbers mean in
  practical terms
- Explain what statistical findings mean rather than just reporting coefficients

**Examples:**

- ❌ **Avoid**: The coefficient was 0.23 (p<0.05), indicating statistical
  significance.

- ✅ **Prefer**: Program participants were 23% more likely to find employment,
  and this difference is unlikely to be due to chance.

- ❌ **Avoid**: The DID estimates were significant (p<0.05) with a coefficient
  of 0.15.

- ✅ **Prefer**: Program participants were 15% more likely to find employment
  compared to similar non-participants, and this difference is unlikely due to
  chance.

## Numbers and Statistics

### General Rules

- **Write out numbers** one through nine; use numerals for 10 and above
- If you have several numbers in the same sentence or paragraph and at least one
  is 10+, write all as numerals
- Avoid starting sentences with a number
  - ❌ NO: Two-thousand-and-two saw the creation of IPA.
  - ✅ YES: In 2002, IPA was created.
- Use numerals for: age, speed, date, time, addresses, percentages, dimensions,
  temperatures, dollars, cents, millions, billions, and in tables and figures

### Percentages

- **Use numerals for percentages**: "15% increase" not "fifteen percent
  increase"
- Always use figures and when possible, the word "percent" (not "per cent")
- In space-constrained contexts such as tables or social media, the % symbol can
  be used

### Decimals and fractions

- **Round appropriately**: Use significance-appropriate precision (e.g., "23.5%"
  not "23.456%")
- Spell out fractions
  - ✅ Yes: two-thirds (note the use of hyphens)
  - ❌ No: 2/3
- Use decimal points when a number cannot be easily written out as a fraction,
  like 1.375 or 47.2

### Age

- Always use numerals
- If the age is used as an adjective or as a substitute for a noun, then it
  should be hyphenated
  - A 21-year-old student
- Age ranges should be written as age 15--19, not aged 15--19 or ages 15--19,
  regardless of context
  - Percentage of children age 6--9 months

### Currency

- Use a numeral followed by the full currency name for the first reference
- Then use the country's ISO abbreviation for secondary references
  - First Reference: The cash transfer value was 5,000 Rwandan francs.
  - Second Reference: The cash transfer value was RWF 5,000.
- You can omit the full currency name for well-known currencies or in
  space-constrained contexts
- If writing for an international audience, convert foreign currency to U.S.
  currency
  - The cash transfer value was 5,000 Rwandan francs (USD 4.72)

### Time

- Use lowercase a.m. and p.m., with periods
- Always use figures, with a space between the time and the a.m. or p.m.: "By
  6:30 a.m. the conference had ended"
- If it's an exact hour, no ":00" is required
- If a time range is entirely in the morning or evening, use a.m. or p.m. only
  once: "6:30-10 p.m."
- If it goes from morning into evening (or vice versa), you need both: "10
  a.m.-2 p.m."
- Specify time zones when writing about an event: 3:00 p.m. EST

### Dates

- Use numerals for days and years
- Write out the month
- Abbreviate only if space is an issue (Jan., Feb., Aug., Sept., Oct., Nov.,
  Dec.)
- When writing in English, use month-day-year format (July 7, 1983)
- The year may be omitted when it is clear to the reader
- Examples:
  - the 1970s (no apostrophe)
  - on the 9th (no superscript)
  - effective 7 July
  - July 1978 (no comma)
  - 1 August 2022 (no 1st)
- When a span of years is indicated, truncate as follows: 1942-45, 1963-70, but
  1995-2002

## Punctuation

Punctuation exists to help the reader understand what you are trying to say. The
general guideline is to use punctuation to make clear the thought being
expressed.

### Periods

- Periods are used at the end of sentences and are followed by a single space
- If a sentence ends with a period that marks an abbreviation or ends with a
  question or exclamation, do not add a second period
  - We have two main offices in the U.S.
  - Can conditional transfers end poverty?

### Commas

The comma usually denotes a slight pause. Use common sense. If you're unsure,
read the sentence out loud. Where you find yourself taking a breath, use a
comma.

- Use a comma when listing three or more items
  - The research proposal included topics such as household food consumption,
    childhood undernutrition, and access to improved seeds.
- In numbers of four or more digits, use commas between groups of three digits,
  counting from the right
  - 32,987
  - 1,512
  - 2,734,456
- Do NOT use commas for years (1995, 2004) or page numbers (pp. 972--1003)

### Semicolon

- Use the semicolon to indicate a greater separation of thought than a comma but
  less than a period
- The two phrases connected by the semicolon should be thematically connected
  - Researchers conducted in-person surveys with participants; they followed up
    three weeks later over the phone.
- Use semicolons to separate elements of a series when the elements are long or
  contain commas
  - Refugees face numerous challenges in their host countries including
    overcoming language barriers, different customs, and negative stereotypes;
    minimal social protection; and poor access to health and sanitation
    services.

### Colon

- Use a colon to give emphasis
  - IPA has a paramount mission: reduce poverty through more evidence.
- Use a colon at the end of a sentence or phrase to introduce lists,
  tabulations, or quoted text
  - Researchers analyzed the following outcomes:
- Use a colon for long passages of dialogue and interviews
  - **Researcher**: How often do you use fertilizer on your crops?
  - **Respondent**: Once, at the beginning of the season.

### Ampersands (&)

- Use the word "and" instead of the ampersand (&), except when it is part of
  official names or abbreviations
  - Spell out "Peace and Recovery" at first mention. Thereafter use "P&R"

### Parentheses

- Avoid parentheses when possible
- Instead rewrite the text or use em dashes or commas to set off information
- If parentheses are required:
  - If the material inside is a complete sentence, the period goes inside the
    close parenthesis
  - If the material inside is not a complete sentence, the period goes outside

### Hyphens

Hyphens are joiners. Use them for:

- Ranges: It takes 20-30 days
- Fractions: Two-thirds
- Phone numbers: 503-494-8311
- Ages when used as adjective: A 21-year-old student
- Use a slash, rather than a hyphen for constructions such as and/or
- Two-word adjectives that precede a noun they modify should generally be
  hyphenated

#### Non-Hyphenated words

- Dropout
- Endline
- Fieldwork
- Healthcare
- Spillovers
- Smallholder
- Policymakers
- Preschool
- Deworming
- Rollout (but "roll out" when used as a verb)
- Turnout (but "turn out" when used as a verb)

#### Hyphenated words

- Cost-sharing
- Cost-effective (always hyphenated)
- Decision-making
- Decision-maker
- School-age
- School-based
- Ultra-poor
- Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs)
- Right-Fit Evidence Unit
- Co-creation
- High-Impact Policy Opportunities

#### Sometimes-hyphenated words (only when used as adjectives)

- Community-based and community-driven
- Follow-up and scale-up
- Short-run and long-run
- Long-term

### Dashes

IPA does not use en dash (--). Use em dash (---) to:

- Set off a series within a phrase
- Indicate a break in thought
- Attribute a quotation to an author
- Note: No spaces on either side of the em-dash

Examples:

- IPA-generated evidence is improving the lives of more than 200 million people
  affected by poverty worldwide---through access to improved social safety nets,
  cash transfers, free health products, and more.
- IPA supports poverty-alleviating policies---when they are backed by evidence.

## Capitalization

Words are lowercase unless a rule says to capitalize them.

- Capitalize the first word of a sentence and the first word of a subparagraph
  or item on a vertical list
- Capitalize proper names. Common nouns are only capitalized when used as part
  of a proper name
  - My friend Annie Duflo is from France.
- Titles are only capitalized if they come directly before a person's name
  - "According to Executive Director Annie Duflo..." but "Annie Duflo is the
    executive director"

### Headings

- For headings, capitalize the first letter of all major words
- Do not capitalize articles (a, an, the), coordinate conjunctions (and, but,
  or), or short prepositions (of, in, at) unless they begin the heading
- For subheadings, capitalize only the first letter
- Abbreviations and most acronyms should be avoided in headlines

## Lists

- Use a colon at the end of the introductory stem sentence
- Capitalize the first word after each bullet or number
- Use periods, not semicolons, after every bullet that is a sentence
- Don't use a period after bullet lists that are not complete sentences or do
  not complete the opening stem sentence
- Use either all full sentences in your bullet lists or all fragments. Avoid a
  mix

Example:

- Researchers analyzed the following outcomes:
  - School attendance
  - Test scores
  - Nutrition
  - Health outcomes

## Quotations

- Use a comma to introduce a direct quotation of one sentence that remains
  within a paragraph
- Use a colon to introduce long quotations and to end all paragraphs that
  introduce a paragraph of quoted material
- Colons go outside quotation marks unless they are part of the quotation itself
- Use "double quote marks" for quotations
- Only use 'inverted commas' for quotations within quotations
- Always place periods and commas inside closing quotation marks, even when they
  are not part of the text being quoted
- Use an ellipsis (...) to indicate the deletion of one or more words in
  condensing quotes
- Be especially careful to avoid deletions that would distort the meaning

## IPA Preferred Language

IPA wants to avoid language that implies or reinforces power dynamics and social
stigmas in research.

### Key Terminology

- ✅ Use "evaluation" instead of "experiment"
- ✅ Use "Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs)" instead of "developing
  country," "third world country," or "Global South"
- ✅ Use "High-Income Countries (HICs)" instead of "Global North"
- ✅ Use [country name] instead of "field"
- ✅ Use "researchers" instead of "principal investigators (PIs)"
- ✅ Use "IPA-affiliated researcher" instead of "IPA researcher"
- ✅ Use "recipients" instead of "beneficiaries"
- ✅ Use "IPA [country name]" instead of "Innovations for Poverty Action
  [country name]" or "[country name] office"
- ✅ Use "participants" instead of "subjects"
- ✅ Use "people living in poverty" instead of "poor people" or "the poor"
- ✅ Use "program" instead of "sector" or "initiatives"
- ✅ Use "informal settlement" instead of "ghetto" or "slum"

### Avoid Stigmatizing Language

Use people-first language:

- ✅ "person with mental illness" not "mentally ill person"
- ✅ "person who uses drugs" not "drug addict"
- ✅ "person living with HIV" not "HIV victim"

### Spelling and Contractions

- Avoid contractions: "it is" not "it's"; "he will" not "he'll"
- Use American spellings: "color" not "colour"; "organize" not "organise"
- Keep British spelling when part of a name: World Food Programme

## Writing About Data

Writing about data is at the core of IPA communications.

### Evidence vs. Data vs. Results

- **Use "evidence"** to refer to a number of rigorous studies that support a
  conclusion
  - "There is strong evidence that cash transfers are associated with a
    reduction in monetary poverty."
- **Use "data"** to refer to raw facts, statistics, and context-free numbers
  - "Data from a digital finance survey showed that one-third of consumers had
    recently experienced overcharges by financial institutions."
- **Use "results"** to refer to the conclusions of a study (impact evaluation,
  analysis, etc.)

### Guidelines for Data

- Study results are considered preliminary until they appear in a published or
  working paper
- IPA does not publish preliminary results
- Be careful when drawing conclusions based on preliminary results and
  generalizing study results in other contexts
- Strive for accuracy and simplicity. Do not obscure analysis with technical
  terms or overstate conclusions
- A number alone does not signify much. It must be compared against something
- Make sure your statement weighs comparable variables and measurement units are
  equal
- Avoid overloading a sentence or paragraph with numbers (rule of thumb: no more
  than 8-10 digits per paragraph)
- When appropriate, use fractions, ratios, and percentages to make numbers
  understandable
- Add appropriate cautions or caveats to numbers
- Include measures of uncertainty like confidence intervals or margin of error
  when appropriate

## Structural Preferences

- **Start with context**: Ground the reader before diving into specifics
- **Use transition phrases**: Help readers follow the logical flow
- **Emphasize key findings**: Make the most important results clear and
  prominent
- **End with implications**: Connect findings to broader significance when
  appropriate

## Section-Specific Guidelines

### Abstract

- **Lead with the main finding**: What is the single most important takeaway?
- **Include key stakeholders**: Who is involved and what role did IPA play?
- **Be specific about outcomes**: Use concrete metrics when available
- **Character limit**: \~350 characters - be concise and impactful

**Example:**

- ❌ **Avoid**: The study examined program effectiveness. Results showed
  improvements.
- ✅ **Prefer**: Researchers evaluated program effectiveness through a
  randomized study. Participants in the program scored 23% higher than similar
  non-participants.

### Introduction

- **Establish relevance**: Why does this research matter?
- **Provide context**: What is the broader setting or problem being addressed?
- **Introduce the intervention**: What program or policy is being studied?
- **Character limit**: \~500 characters - set the stage efficiently

### About the Project

- **Highlight IPA's role**: Be specific about IPA's contribution
- **Describe methodology clearly**: Explain the research design accessibly
- **Include sample details**: Provide key information about who participated
- **Avoid technical jargon**: Use plain language to describe the approach
- **Character limit**: 500-800 characters - balance detail with brevity

### Main Results

- **Lead with headline findings**: What are the most important results?
- **Translate statistics**: Explain what the numbers mean in practical terms
- **Note unexpected findings**: Include surprising null results if important
- **Use accessible language**: No statistical jargon without explanation
- **Character limit**: 500-800 characters - be comprehensive but concise

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

### Don't

- ❌ Use passive voice excessively: "The intervention was implemented by
  researchers"
- ❌ Use unexplained jargon: "The DID estimator showed..."
- ❌ Bury the lede: Put important findings up front
- ❌ Use vague language: "Some participants showed improvements"
- ❌ Overuse qualifiers: "perhaps," "possibly," "might" (when evidence is clear)
- ❌ Use "treatment" and "control" without context
- ❌ Start sentences with numbers: "2002 saw the creation of IPA"
- ❌ Use contractions or informal language
- ❌ Use stigmatizing or deficit-based language
- ❌ Overload paragraphs with too many numbers

### Do

- ✅ Use active voice: "Researchers implemented the intervention"
- ✅ Explain terms: "Using difference-in-differences analysis (comparing changes
  over time)..."
- ✅ Lead with findings: "Students improved reading scores by 15%"
- ✅ Be specific: "45% of participants showed improved test scores"
- ✅ State findings confidently: "The program increased employment by 12%"
- ✅ Use descriptive terms: "students enrolled in the program" vs. "treatment
  students"
- ✅ Provide context for all statistics
- ✅ Use people-first, respectful language
- ✅ Show evidence rather than just telling
- ✅ Maintain consistent tone and style throughout

## Examples

### Example 1: Active vs. Passive Voice

**Avoid**: "A randomized evaluation was conducted to assess the effectiveness of
the program."

**Prefer**: "Researchers conducted a randomized evaluation to assess the
program's effectiveness."

### Example 2: Explaining Technical Terms

**Avoid**: "Using a DID approach with PSM, researchers found significant ATEs."

**Prefer**: "Researchers used statistical methods to compare changes over time
between program participants and similar non-participants. They found that the
program had significant positive effects."

### Example 3: Avoiding Treatment/Control Language

**Avoid**: "Treatment group students outperformed control group students by
20%."

**Prefer**: "Students enrolled in the program scored 20% higher than similar
students who did not participate."

### Example 4: Translating Statistics

**Avoid**: "The coefficient was 0.23 (p<0.05), indicating statistical
significance."

**Prefer**: "Program participants were 23% more likely to find employment, and
this difference is unlikely to be due to chance."

### Example 5: Being Concise

**Avoid**: "To support national-level policy development and program design in
the places where we work, IPA leverages our evidence expertise to provide
actionable lessons from relevant research by synthesizing evidence in clear
terms and bringing key insights to inform decision-making."

**Prefer**: "IPA synthesizes evidence to provide key insights and actionable
lessons for policy and program development."

### Example 6: Show, Don't Tell

**Avoid**: "Data collection was challenging given the village conditions."

**Prefer**: "The village was located in a remote area. To collect data,
surveyors had to take a road whose poor conditions turned a thirty-minute trip
into a four-hour trip."

## Citations and References

IPA uses the *Chicago Manual of Style*'s author-date form for citations.
Footnotes should be avoided when possible.

### In-text citations

- (Keng, Lin, and Orazem 2017, 9--10)
- (Bay et al. 2017, 465)

### Reference list entries

- Include full bibliographic information
- Use DOI links when available (<https://doi.org/>)
- For web sources, include access dates if no publication date

## Guidelines Enforcement

These guidelines are applied during the summary generation process. The LLM
receives these instructions as part of its system prompt and will follow them
while maintaining the required summary structure and character limits.

**Enforcement Level**: Configurable via `.env` file

- `strict`: Guidelines are mandatory requirements
- `moderate`: Guidelines are strong recommendations
- `loose`: Guidelines are suggestions for improvement
